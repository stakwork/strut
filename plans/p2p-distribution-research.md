# Hash-named packages dissolve the fork treadmill

Strut can get out of the pull-from-upstream / send-a-PR loop without adopting any single peer-to-peer network, because the research shows the "no maintenance" property of p2p systems is not a network at all but four separable mechanisms: signed records anyone may republish, stateless relays that cache, content addressing so bytes can come from whoever has them, and bearer tokens a server verifies with a key it already holds. Strut already has the hardest two (every version is a sha256 tree hash with recorded `upstream`; LLM spend is governed by macaroons verifiable from a public key), so the fork problem is best dissolved the way Unison and Nix dissolve it — **a name is per-node metadata pointing at a hash, two versions coexist, and "update" is a typed re-pointing step** — rather than by a smarter merge. On top of that spine, six architectures were assessed. For announcing and finding packages, Nostr's addressable events plus Blossom's by-sha256 blobs are the closest fit to what strut already does (HTTP by hash, Ed25519/secp256k1 keys) and have mature TypeScript libraries, but Nostr has no key rotation and relays promise no retention. For laptops behind NAT, Pkarr on the Mainline DHT plus one or two stateless relays (iroh, or Pear's blind relays) is the minimal infrastructure, at the cost of an hourly re-put someone must run. CRDTs and commutative patches work for Concept pages and metadata and fail for code without a review step, which Ink & Switch's own retrospective concedes. Where money actually flows in every agent ecosystem with public numbers is **metered hosted execution**, not package sales — Apify takes 20% of publisher-defined billable events while the Virtuals agent marketplace fell from **$345,197 in platform fees in March 2026 to $5.48 in September** — so strut's already-built run-on-a-peer path, gated by an L402-shaped macaroon (verified with no database and no third party), is the revenue mechanism with the strongest precedent, and it sits beside the package plan without changing §1–§5. The recommendation is staged: make coexistence the normal state of a fork now, add signed release announcements and by-hash fetch second, charge per run with L402 third, and keep the "no public index yet" ruling until a run-backed track record exists to put in one. Two rulings would need revisiting: "what is sold is packages" (paid execution moves forward) and, if Nostr relays carry announcements, "no public index yet" (a public relay is an index by nature; an org relay is not).

## The no-maintenance trick is four mechanisms, not a network

Every surviving p2p system in the notes converges on the same shape, and none of them achieves "publish once, available forever, zero work" literally. BEP 44 on the Mainline DHT says items "MAY expire after 2 hours" and "SHOULD be re-announced once per hour", but adds the sentence that makes the whole design work: "Anyone may republish an item without the private key by repeating the original signed put. This keeps data alive after the publisher goes offline" ([BEP 44](https://www.bittorrent.org/beps/bep_0044.html)). IPNS has the same property — DHT peers keep records at most 48 hours, and a still-valid signed record can be re-published by anyone through `PUT /routing/v1/ipns` on a delegated routing endpoint, so the community pattern is "set validity to a year, then cron a republish" ([IPNS record spec](https://specs.ipfs.tech/ipns/ipns-record/), [discuss.ipfs.tech](https://discuss.ipfs.tech/t/ipns-publishing-guidance-for-high-availability/18928)). Pkarr says it plainly: "the DHT drops records after hours, so you must republish periodically", and its relays "cache aggressively" and republish for their users ([Pubky/pkarr](https://github.com/Pubky/pkarr), [relays.md](https://github.com/Pubky/pkarr/blob/main/design/relays.md)). **Mechanism one is a signed record whose freshness is a monotonic counter and which any reader may re-put** — readers become the keepalive, so popular things stay alive and dead things expire with no operator action.

Mechanism two is the stateless relay. Secure Scuttlebutt's history is the clearest natural experiment: pubs (stateful servers holding copies) decayed to month-long outages and Manyverse stopped development, while rooms, which "do not store any user data" and only introduce online friends, were the part that survived ([Announcing SSB Rooms](https://manyver.se/blog/announcing-ssb-rooms), [AlternativeTo](https://alternativeto.net/software/manyverse/about)). iroh's relays "are stateless and don't store application data" and carry traffic only when hole punching fails ([iroh relays](https://docs.iroh.computer/concepts/relays)); Pear's blind relay "forwards connections and does not store data" ([Peer-to-peer, demystified](https://docs.pears.com/explanation/peer-to-peer-demystified/)). Stateful holders — pinning services, seeders — are where "constant maintenance" reappears: four years after publishing a site to IPFS, Dries Buytaert found "half the services have shut down, restricted access, or pivoted entirely", his verdict being "technically robust, commercially fragile" ([dri.es](https://dri.es/my-web3-site-survived-four-years-of-neglect)).

Mechanism three is content addressing, which turns "where do I fetch from" into "who has this hash". Blossom serves blobs at `GET /<sha256>` and lets any party `PUT /mirror` a blob onto another server without the publisher's cooperation; BUD-03 tells a client that finds a dead URL to extract the 64-hex hash, read the author's server list, and try each server in order ([Blossom README](https://github.com/hzrd149/blossom/blob/master/README.md), [BUD-03](https://github.com/hzrd149/blossom/blob/master/buds/03.md)). Dhall's hash-pinned imports are "permanently locally cached after the first request" so later evaluations make no outbound request at all ([Dhall safety guarantees](https://docs.dhall-lang.org/discussions/Safety-guarantees.html)). The IPFS trustless gateway inverts the usual server role: a gateway without the content "should return 404 Not Found rather than fetch it elsewhere" — the client verifies every block and discovery is someone else's job ([Trustless Gateway spec](https://specs.ipfs.tech/http-gateways/trustless-gateway/)). Strut's tree hash fits any of these; the mutable-pointer records are tiny everywhere (≤1000 bytes for BEP 44, ≤10 KiB for IPNS, ~1 KB for Pkarr) and the bytes always travel separately.

Mechanism four is the offline-verifiable token. L402's macaroon "commits to the Lightning invoice by containing the invoice's payment hash" and servers verify "using only the macaroon and preimage" with no database lookup ([L402 overview](https://docs.lightning.engineering/the-lightning-network/l402), [L402 README](https://github.com/lightninglabs/L402)). Biscuit goes one step further: public-key signed, so "a reseller or a second server can verify without the issuer's secret", with verify plus policy under a millisecond ([Clever Cloud](https://www.clever-cloud.com/blog/engineering/2021/04/12/introduction-to-biscuit/)). Strut's file token and its gatekey macaroons are already this mechanism; what the research adds is that the same token can gate a package download at a mirror, a per-run launch, and a year of updates, with revocation as a denylist of user ids or a root-key rotation ([Macaroons](https://docs.lightning.engineering/the-lightning-network/l402/macaroons)).

The honest corollary: a laptop strut online a few hours a day cannot be the sole keeper of any record, so every option below needs either a cloud strut, a relay, or reader-side republish; and hole punching fails for a predictable tail — **~70% success for libp2p in the wild** from 4.4 million attempts, "well north of 90%" for Tailscale by its own internal metrics — so relay fallback is a normal path with real bandwidth, not an exception ([Trautwein et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27500v1), [Tailscale](https://tailscale.com/blog/nat-traversal-improvements-pt-1)).

## Option A — names are metadata, versions coexist, and the fork stops being an event

**The idea.** In Unison "each Unison definition is identified by a hash of its syntax tree" and "names are just separately stored metadata that don't affect the function's hash"; a rename changes only the name→hash mapping, and "it's perfectly fine to have two different Email types floating around the codebase", consolidated later or never ([Unison: The big idea](https://www.unison-lang.org/docs/the-big-idea/)). Applied to strut: every workflow, step, Concept and app version is already a hash; the change is to stop treating a local edit as a *fork of a package* and treat it as *another hash with an edge to its parent*. Upstream publishing a new hash changes nothing on your strut until you run an `upgrade`-like step that re-points your name and re-validates what depends on it. There is no PR because there is no branch owner to petition: your variant is published as a value, and anyone — including the original publisher — may re-point their name to it. Nix overlays are the package-set form of the same idea: a `final: prev:` function overrides a definition without copying upstream, so upstream and your tweak stay separate layers ([Nixpkgs overlays](https://nixos.org/manual/nixpkgs/stable/#chap-overlays)). Fossil's model is the social form: two commits against the same check-in produce a fork whose leaves simply coexist until someone chooses to merge ([Fossil concepts](https://fossil-scm.org/home/doc/trunk/www/concepts.wiki)).

**What makes propagation a non-event rather than a merge** is Unison's typed `update`: when an edit is type-preserving, "your change propagates automatically and doesn't create a branch"; otherwise affected terms go to a scratch file on a temporary branch and the codebase "always typechecks, even partway through a complex refactoring" ([Unison: improved update process](https://www.unison-lang.org/blog/new-update-process/)). `lib.install` puts a new library version in as "a sibling of your current library version", and `upgrade <old> <new>` runs on both ([Unison: lib.install](https://www.unison-lang.org/docs/usage-topics/workflow-how-tos/update-dependency-lib-install/)). Strut's steps carry Zod schemas, workflows reference steps by name or pinned `type@vN`, and `flowClosure` already knows what a workflow executes — so a schema diff can play the type-check's role: an upstream step whose input/output schema is unchanged can re-point silently under every workflow that names it; one whose schema changed lands beside the old version and the dependents are listed for the builder. Elm already does the compiler-decides-the-bump half: `elm bump` computes MAJOR versus MINOR from the API diff ([elm.json package docs](https://github.com/elm/compiler/blob/master/docs/elm.json/package.md)).

**How it serves the four needs.** (1) Distribute like git: better than git for the owner's complaint, because there is no rebase — a fork is a leaf, and a pull is a re-point with a check. (2) Share patterns: a pattern is a hash plus the names people give it; curation is a name→hash list, which is exactly what Nix's `flake-registry.json` (one JSON file mapping short names to flake URIs) and OCI tags (mutable pointers, several per digest) are ([NixOS/flake-registry](https://github.com/NixOS/flake-registry), [OCI distribution spec](https://github.com/opencontainers/distribution-spec/blob/main/spec.md)). (3) Sell packages: neutral, but it sharpens what is bought — a hash and a right to fetch later hashes under a name, not a branch; §6's token scope `package:acme/linear` plus an expiry is unchanged. (4) Charge per call: neutral.

**Who decides "latest" is the open design choice, and the research gives three answers.** One owner (Unison's codebase, an OCI tag pusher, a NIP-34 announcer whose "most recent Status event" is current), a quorum (Radicle: "with 2-of-3 delegates, a commit that two delegates have pushed to their master branches becomes canonical", generalized in August 2025 to per-reference rules with allowed DIDs and a `threshold` — the only hub-free multi-party "canonical" in the set), or nobody (Fossil leaves) ([Radicle protocol guide](https://radicle.dev/guides/protocol), [Canonical References](https://radicle.dev/2025/08/12/canonical-references.html), [NIP-34](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/34.md)). For a single publisher, the publisher's signed pointer is enough; for an org's shared workflow, Radicle's identity-doc-with-threshold is the borrowable primitive. Radicle itself is shipping (1.10.3 released 2026-09-08) but had two vulnerability disclosures in 2026 ([radicle.dev](https://radicle.dev/)).

**What it changes in the plan.** Less than it sounds. §1's digest and §3's `upstream` stamp are already the hash-identity half. §4's rule "a modified one gets the new version beside it, with the local edit left active" stops being the exception row of a table and becomes the whole model; "rebase is the builder's" stays as the tool for when someone *wants* to converge, not the thing an update implies. What is added: a per-node name→hash map that is openly per-node (the plan's `vN` labels already are, and the notes' Unison lesson is that the label "must never be the identity that crosses nodes"); a schema-diff check on re-point that classifies an update as silent or needs-a-person before it asks; and a way to publish a variant *as a value* without a PR — which is what §5's "exporting is the other direction, for git … always a PR" currently forecloses, and what Options B and C supply transport for. The sealed and `publisher: ai` stamps carry through unchanged because they ride on the hash.

**Maintenance.** None beyond what strut runs today; this is a data model, not a network. **Risks.** Unison's own retrospective: the old process let names be "accidentally replace[d] … with mysterious hashes" with "no indication that conflicts are present unless you run `todo`" — the UI has to keep hashes behind names and surface divergence without being asked ([Unison blog](https://www.unison-lang.org/blog/new-update-process/)). Nix's content-addressed derivations are a warning about the trust split: CA paths need no signature but *realisations* (the claim that this input produced this output) do, and the feature has sat experimental since Nix 2.4 with no 2026 stabilization found ([RFC 0062](https://github.com/NixOS/rfcs/blob/master/rfcs/0062-content-addressed-paths.md), [NixOS wiki](https://wiki.nixos.org/wiki/Ca-derivations)). **Maturity call: idea to borrow, not code to adopt** — nothing ships a Node/TypeScript library for content-addressed code with typed propagation; that combination exists only inside UCM, which is Haskell. Gap: the notes could not retrieve Unison's merge internals or how Share designates a release.

## Option B — Nostr for publish, discover and pay: Zapstore's three events, Blossom's blobs, zaps and a 402

**The idea.** A strut's publishing identity is a secp256k1 keypair. A package release is three signed events in the shape Zapstore already uses for Android apps: **kind 32267** Software Application (name, summary, icon, repository), **kind 30063** Software Release with `d` = `<app-id>@<version>`, and **kind 3063** Software Asset carrying the `x` sha256 and a URL; assets go to Blossom at `<server>/<sha256>` or a GitHub release ([zapstore/zsp](https://github.com/zapstore/zsp)). NIP-51 already standardizes **30063 release artifact sets** and **30267 app curation sets** as list kinds, and NIP-94 kind 1063 file metadata carries `x`, `m`, `size`, `magnet` ([NIP-51](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/51.md), [NIP-94](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/94.md)). Because addressable kinds 30000–39999 keep one event per (pubkey, kind, `d`) with the newest `created_at` winning, "latest of package X from publisher P" is a stable triple, and a per-version `d` gives history too ([NIP-01](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/01.md)). Discovery is social: the outbox model (kind 10002 lists a user's write relays; readers fetch from them), NIP-89 recommendations from accounts you follow, and web-of-trust scoring either as pre-published NIP-85 assertions or as a query to a service like Vertex, which runs personalized PageRank over the follow graph with 100 free credits a day ([NIP-65](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/65.md), [NIP-89](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/89.md), [vertexlab.io](https://vertexlab.io/docs/faq)). The strut tree hash is the `x` tag; the archive route strut already has is a Blossom server in all but name — `GET /<sha256>` with the digest in a header.

**How it serves the four needs.** (1) Distribute like git without the forge: a release is a broadcast value; a fork is your own 30063 under your key with the parent's event referenced; NIP-34 shows the same network carrying git itself — kind 30617 repo announcements with `euc` ("the commit ID of the earliest unique commit") to group forks, 1617 patches as `format-patch` text, 1618 PRs, and status events from a maintainer rather than a merge button, with ngit 3.0.0 live on crates.io ([NIP-34](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/34.md), [docs.rs ngit](https://docs.rs/crate/ngit/3.0.0)). (2) Share patterns: curation sets (30004, 30267) from people you follow are the "cool patterns" feed, and a Concept page is a note. (3) Sell packages: three mechanisms exist with different trust. Zaps (NIP-57, a payer-signed 9734 request and a 9735 receipt from the recipient's LNURL server) are payment-backed signals and tips, not entitlements — Zapstore's page shows 108,632,063 sats sent through the store ([NIP-57](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/57.md), [OpenSats Zapstore](https://opensats.org/projects/zapstore)). An L402 on the archive route is the entitlement (below). Cashu NUT-24 puts a bearer ecash token in an `X-Cashu` header after a 402 — fine granularity (`u` may be `sat`, `usd` or `api`) but the seller must swap it at a mint it listed and trust that mint's solvency, and `@cashu/cashu-ts` shows NUT-24 support unchecked ([NUT-24](https://github.com/cashubtc/nuts/blob/main/24.md), [cashu-ts](https://classic.yarnpkg.com/en/package/@cashu/cashu-ts)). (4) Charge per call: NIP-90 Data Vending Machines were the designed answer — job kinds 5000–5999, `bid` in msats, `payment-required` feedback — and the spec header now marks NIP-90 "draft" and "unrecommended", suggesting use-case microstandards instead; one provider "waited eight hours without a single job request", blaming client discovery and very low pricing ([NIP-90](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/90.md), [stacker.news](https://www.stacker.news/items/1463738)). The one durable DVM business is web-of-trust scoring. So Nostr is where the *announcement* of a paid endpoint lives; the charging itself belongs to Option E's 402.

**2026 status.** Zapstore 1.0 shipped for Android with iOS in development, OpenSats-funded, MIT, and it verifies the APK hash and signing certificate against the signed 3063 event before install ([zapstore/zapstore](https://github.com/zapstore/zapstore), [OpenSats](https://opensats.org/projects/zapstore)). Two honest caveats come with it: in the 2024 alpha "app releases are signed/curated by this account, as developers start self-signing" — trust was bootstrapped by one curator — and Zapstore runs its own relay (`wss://relay.zapstore.dev`) and CDN, so "someone else runs it" was the store project ([nobsbitcoin](https://nobsbitcoin.com/zap-store), [zsp](https://github.com/zapstore/zsp)). The "NIP-82" label for these kinds is the project's own; the notes did not find it merged into nostr-protocol/nips. Libraries are the strongest of any option: `nostr-tools` 2.25.2 with ~35 NIP modules and only `@noble`/`@scure` dependencies, NDK 2.14.33 with the outbox model built in, `blossom-client-sdk` 5.1.0, and a JSR Blossom server ([nbd-wtf/nostr-tools](https://github.com/nbd-wtf/nostr-tools), [NDK](https://unpkg.com/@nostr-dev-kit/ndk@2.14.33/README.md), [npm blossom-client-sdk](https://www.npmjs.com/package/blossom-client-sdk)).

**What it changes in the plan.** §5 gains a `nostr:` resolver: an `naddr` or `<pubkey>/<package>` resolves to the newest 30063 on the publisher's write relays, the 3063 gives the `x` hash and URLs, Blossom-style fetch by hash with BUD-03 fallback, then the existing `installPackage` from a directory. §7's index becomes optional: "a peer's `GET /packages`" is still a list, and a curation set from followed keys is another list; `index.yaml` in git stays for deployments that want nothing social. §6's archive route adds a 402. **The ruling to revisit is "no public index yet"**: a public relay is a public index by construction. The compatible form is an org-run relay (Zapstore ships "a Nostr relay and Blossom server with Bunny CDN integration" in Go; Route96 is a self-hosted Blossom+NIP-96 server on Umbrel), so a deployment names its relays the way it names its indexes today ([pkg.go.dev zapstore/relay](https://pkg.go.dev/github.com/zapstore/relay), [apps.umbrel.com](https://apps.umbrel.com/app/route96)). "Installing is a person's act" and "updates ask" hold unchanged — a release event is an announcement, not an install.

**Maintenance.** The publisher runs nothing, which is the pitch; what it assumes is relays that keep events and a Blossom server that keeps bytes, and neither promises to. NIP-11 lets a relay declare `retention` (null infinite, 0 not stored) and notes "there may be a cost associated with storing data forever"; a paid relay charges a prorated annual fee per note and contrasts itself with relays that "periodically purge old notes" ([NIP-11](https://nips.4rs.nl/nips/11), [docs.nostr.wine](https://docs.nostr.wine/cellar/readme)). Mitigation is the same as everywhere: an org relay or a paid one, and strut's own archive route as the Blossom server of last resort. **Risks.** No key rotation: a July 2026 guide found the often-cited "NIP-41" returns 404 and "a compromised Nostr key cannot be rotated or revoked… no shipped mechanism, no merged spec, and no rough consensus" ([D-Central](https://d-central.tech/nostr-key-management/)); the mitigation is NIP-46 remote signing so the publishing tool never holds the key, and keeping strut's peer record (base URL + token, pasted by a person) as the trust root that says which key is current. A stale-replaceable-event hole: a relay can serve an older 30063 and the client cannot tell without querying several relays; the notes found no source on how clients defend beyond multi-relay reads. Spam: NIP-89 itself warns that querying handlers by kind without a follow-graph filter "carries spam and malicious-handler risks" ([NIP-89](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/89.md)). **Maturity call: adoptable now for announce + fetch + tip; mature libraries; social discovery proven at Zapstore's scale; identity recovery unsolved; not a per-call payment rail.**

## Option C — a DHT plus one or two stateless relays: Pkarr, iroh, Hyperswarm

**The idea.** Replace "a peer is a base URL" with "a peer is a public key", and let the Mainline DHT answer two questions no one has to run a server for: where is strut X right now, and what is the latest of package Y under key K. Pkarr makes an Ed25519 key a DNS top-level domain: the owner signs a compressed DNS packet under 1000 bytes, serialized as pubkey + signature + microsecond timestamp + packet, and publishes it as a BEP 44 mutable item with `seq` = timestamp; resolvers accept only a newer timestamp ([pkarr base.md](https://github.com/Pubky/pkarr/blob/main/design/base.md)). A TXT record under `<package>.<pubkey>` can carry a tree hash and a URL list; HTTP relays (`PUT /:z32key`, `GET /:z32key` with `Cache-Control` and `If-Modified-Since`) serve browsers and firewalled hosts and verify the signature before putting ([relays.md](https://github.com/Pubky/pkarr/blob/main/design/relays.md)). For the connection itself, iroh dials "p2p quic connections dialed by public key": relays are used at every setup to exchange addresses and attempt a direct connection, carry traffic only if punching fails, "cannot read any of the traffic they handle", and are stateless; discovery defaults to a Pkarr packet on n0's DNS server with Mainline publishing opt-in ([iroh relays](https://docs.iroh.computer/concepts/relays), [iroh discovery](https://docs.iroh.computer/concepts/discovery)). The alternative stack is pure JavaScript: Hyperswarm/HyperDHT (keyed peer lookup, hole punching, `mutablePut(keyPair, value, { seq })` BEP 44-style records) and Pear's release pipeline, which is package distribution already — `pear stage` appends a directory into Hypercore storage, a versioned `pear://` link "pins fork, length, and key", production writes go through multisig with a quorum "so one compromised machine can't redefine the release line", and the updater streams new blocks and swaps the app directory ([HyperDHT docs](https://docs.pears.com/building-blocks/hyperdht), [Release pipeline](https://docs.pears.com/explanation/release-pipeline/)).

**How it serves the four needs.** (1) Distribute like git: yes for the bytes — any strut that installed a package can serve it by hash, and the pointer record survives the publisher's laptop sleeping as long as some reader or relay re-puts it. (2) Share patterns: weak. A DHT is a lookup, not a search; names are keys, and there is nothing to browse without a social or index layer on top — the Mainline DHT has no equivalent of Nostr's curation sets. (3) Sell packages: unchanged from the plan — the token on the archive route works whether the route is reached by URL or by key. (4) Charge per call: nothing here; a DHT carries pointers, not invoices.

**2026 status.** Pkarr: Rust crate 8.1.0 and `@synonymdev/pkarr` 8.1.0 (JS/WASM, Node and browsers, no native build) both dated 2026-10-02; iroh's default discovery uses the Pkarr format, which is the adoption signal ([docs.rs pkarr](https://docs.rs/crate/pkarr/latest), [npm @synonymdev/pkarr](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@synonymdev/pkarr), [The road to iroh 1.0](https://www.iroh.computer/blog/the-road-to-iroh-1-0)). iroh: 1.0 shipped mid-2026 (n0's post is dated July 9; third-party coverage says June 15 — the dates conflict), crate 1.4.1 on 2026-10-21 with ~596k downloads a month, `@number0/iroh` 1.2.0 napi-rs binding for Node ≥ 20.3 (macOS arm64, Linux, Windows; no Intel-mac prebuilt); but the current `iroh-blobs` README says it "is not yet considered production quality" and recommends 0.35, and n0 publishes no hole-punching success rate while citing libp2p's 70% as the figure it beats ([lib.rs/crates/iroh](https://lib.rs/crates/iroh), [npm @number0/iroh](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@number0/iroh), [iroh-blobs README](https://github.com/n0-computer/iroh-blobs)). Holepunch: `hyperswarm` 4.17.2, `hyperdht` 6.34.1, `hypercore` 11.39.3, `autobase` 7.28.2, all released in September–October 2026 — the only stack that is pure JS, released weekly, and already shaped like package distribution; the docs are candid that hole punching "is not guaranteed on every corporate or symmetric-NAT setup" and that "a new user can only download data that at least one online peer already holds" ([npm hyperswarm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/hyperswarm), [Peer-to-peer, demystified](https://docs.pears.com/explanation/peer-to-peer-demystified/), [Availability and blind peering](https://docs.pears.com/explanation/availability-and-blind-peering/)). The Mainline DHT's only size figure is from 2013 (15–27 million nodes a day); no 2024–2026 measurement surfaced, which is a gap ([Cambridge MLDHT](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~lw525/MLDHT/)).

**What it changes in the plan.** A `pk:` source beside `peer:`: `pk:<z32 key>/<publisher>/<name>` resolves the signed packet, reads the tree hash and URL list, fetches by hash from the first reachable holder. The peer record grows an optional public key so a desktop strut behind NAT is reachable from its cloud strut without a pasted URL — the use case federation already names. The archive route stays the byte server; iroh or Hyperswarm would replace *how* two struts connect, not what they say. Nothing about install, asking, or forks changes.

**Maintenance.** One or two relays (iroh-relay, a Pear blind relay, or a Pkarr HTTP relay) that store nothing, plus an hourly re-put of each pointer by whoever is online — in practice the cloud strut. At 10–10,000 struts the relay bandwidth is small because package bundles are small and most struts can fetch from a cloud strut directly; the notes found no relay cost figures at this scale. **Risks.** Key loss is unsolved in every spec here — BEP 44 "does not describe key rotation", Pkarr's design "does not address it" — so the only mitigations in the wild are multisig (Pear), a human-readable indirection that can be re-pointed, or strut's own peer record as the root ([BEP 44](https://www.bittorrent.org/beps/bep_0044.html), [pkarr base.md](https://github.com/Pubky/pkarr/blob/main/design/base.md)). A ≤1000-byte value, one UDP response per query with "no retry", and BEP 50's warning to poll mutable items at a low rate bound what the DHT can carry ([BEP 5](https://www.bittorrent.org/beps/bep_0005.html)). Browser peers cannot reach ordinary peers ([WebTorrent FAQ](https://webtorrent.io/faq)). A third-party analysis notes that by default "you have to trust iroh's discovery layer (n0's public pkarr nodes and n0's public relay; self-hosting requires extra deployment)" ([Mi&Bee](https://blog.mickeyzzc.tech/en/posts/network/iroh-project-deep-analysis/)). **Maturity call: Pkarr and Hypercore are stable wire formats with current libraries and are adoptable; iroh's transport is 1.0 but its blob layer is mid-rewrite; adopt for reachability and pointers, not for discovery or money.**

## Option D — commutative patches and CRDTs: right for Concepts and metadata, wrong as the merge engine for code

**The idea.** Make divergence disappear by changing the algebra of edits rather than the identity of versions. In Pijul "for any two changes A and B, either A and B can be applied in any order, or A depends on B, or B depends on A"; conflicts are stored in the pristine and both sides' edits are applied unresolved, which "guarantees no information ever gets lost", and "a resolution is itself a change that applies to the same two changes everywhere" ([Pijul manual](https://pijul.org/manual/why_pijul.html), [pijul.org](https://pijul.org/)). That last property is the one strut would want: a fix to a shared step that one strut resolved is a value another strut can apply without a PR. CRDTs are the data version: Automerge 3.0 cut memory "by over 10x" by keeping the compressed representation at runtime, 3.5.0 added authorship in September 2026, and Keyhive adds "convergent capabilities" so an untrusted sync server can hold ciphertext it cannot read ([Automerge 3.0](https://automerge.org/blog/automerge-3/), [Automerge blog](https://automerge.org/blog/), [Keyhive notebook](https://www.inkandswitch.com/keyhive/notebook/)). Radicle already uses the CRDT shape for collaboration objects — issues and patches are signed commit DAGs where "peers sync by unioning commit graphs, which is idempotent and CRDT-like" ([Radicle protocol guide](https://radicle.dev/guides/protocol)).

**Where it fails for code, in the researchers' own words.** Ink & Switch's Upwelling, a document version-control system on Automerge, names the failure modes: syntactic merges "in crude ways" (two edits of "white" yielding "frostysoft"), deleted paragraphs leaving inserted text behind, and "semantic conflicts cannot always be detected, human review of the merged result is necessary in any case"; the conclusion is "automatic merging is necessary but not sufficient", and the right unit is drafts, not keystrokes ([Upwelling](https://www.inkandswitch.com/upwelling/)). For a YAML workflow or a TypeScript step the semantic conflict is the whole problem; a merged step that parses and fails its Zod schema or its claims is worse than a conflict marker. The agent-ecosystem precedents add a second objection: silently merging an upstream change into something you already approved is what the Cloud Security Alliance calls the MCP **rug pull** — "a server ships a benign version that users install and approve, then pushes an update with a poisoned description" — which "defeats point-in-time security reviews", and the recommended defense is tool-definition hash pinning ([CSA whitepaper](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research-rb/csa-whitepaper-mcp-security-tool-poisoning-20260506-csa-styl/)). Automatic convergence of code collides with the owner's own ruling that new code never lands on an accept given for other code; Ink & Switch's Patchwork found that even for prose, writers wanted an explicit "branch" they merge on purpose ([Patchwork notebook](https://www.inkandswitch.com/patchwork/notebook/2024-version-control/06/)).

**How it serves the four needs.** (1) Distribute like git: partially — patches-as-values remove the PR for *sharing a fix*, but the receiving strut still needs a person and a check before activation. (2) Share patterns: strongly, for the things that are prose and lists — Concept pages, curation sets, claims text, an index entry — where concurrent edits from several struts' builders are expected and a last-writer-wins would lose work. (3) and (4): nothing.

**2026 status.** Pijul's install line is still `cargo install pijul --version "~1.0.0-beta"`; the 1.0 beta was announced January 2022, the last packaged beta is beta.9 from May 2024, and no 2025–2026 release was found; the Nest hosting platform was announced as moving to a "serverless" redesign after a datacenter fire ([pijul.org](https://pijul.org/), [SUSE package](https://packagehub.suse.com/packages/pijul/1_0_0-beta_9-bp160_1_15)). Darcs 2.18.5 shipped "mostly because some dependencies made breaking changes" ([Darcs bug tracker](https://bugs.darcs.net/patch2451)). Automerge is mature (JS/wasm and Rust, `@automerge/automerge-repo` 2.1.0); Keyhive/Beelay are pre-alpha crates from March 2025, "not for production", not audited; Loro has Rust, JS/wasm and Swift ([Keyhive notebook](https://www.inkandswitch.com/keyhive/notebook/), [loro-dev/loro](https://github.com/loro-dev/loro)). Braid-HTTP (versioning, subscriptions and patches on GET) is an IETF draft with biweekly meetings through 2026 and alpha libraries ([braid.org](https://braid.org/)). The notes found no primary source evaluating CRDT merges on source code specifically — a gap worth stating.

**What it changes in the plan.** Nothing in §4's code path: `git merge-file` on base/theirs/ours with the builder resolving markers and the claims re-running is the right mechanism, and the research strengthens it. Two additions: Concept pages (and later a shared index or curation list) could be Automerge documents synced between an org's struts so several builders' edits converge, with the `unique_source_id` stamp still saying which package they came from; and a "patch as a value" door — publish the diff a fork made as a signed object another strut may apply through the same ask-then-merge path — which is NIP-34's 1617 patch event if Option B is adopted. **Maintenance.** A sync server or relay for the CRDT documents, or piggyback on whatever Option B/C provides. **Risks.** Pijul is a one-maintainer project in beta for four years; Keyhive is research. **Maturity call: adopt Automerge for prose and lists if co-editing across struts becomes real; borrow Pijul's "resolution is a shareable change" as an idea; never let a CRDT activate code.**

## Option E — a hosted service you call, not code you copy: run-on-a-peer with a 402 in front

**The idea.** The owner's fourth need, and the one every agent marketplace with public numbers monetizes, is not a package at all: the workflow stays on the seller's strut, the buyer launches it there and tails the result, and the launch route charges. Strut has built the first half — `strut/run-workflow` launches `POST {peer}/workflows/:name/run` with the caller's principal as `x-strut-actor`, waits on the peer's SSE tail with `?skip=N` reattach, cancels through, and the peer runs only a workflow whose YAML says `peer: true`, as published, with no `params`. What is missing is the price. L402 supplies it in the shape strut already understands: the server answers `402` with `WWW-Authenticate: L402 macaroon="…", invoice="<bolt11>"`; the client pays, learns the preimage, and retries with `Authorization: L402 <macaroon>:<preimage>`; the server verifies the HMAC chain and `sha256(preimage) == payment_hash` with its root key alone — "using only a root key and basic cryptography", no payment database, no third party ([L402 protocol specification](https://docs.lightning.engineering/the-lightning-network/l402/protocol-specification), [L402 overview](https://docs.lightning.engineering/the-lightning-network/l402)). Caveats are strings appended to the macaroon; Lightning Loop's real ones include `services = lightning_loop:0` and `loop_out_monthly_volume_sats = 200000000`, and "anyone holding a macaroon can add a caveat" to attenuate it for a sub-agent ([Macaroons](https://docs.lightning.engineering/the-lightning-network/l402/macaroons)). Strut's gatekey macaroons, its per-run and per-step HMAC links, and its `maxRunCostUsd` cap are this exact mechanism pointed at the LLM gateway; pointed at the launch route they become `workflow=acme/linear/sync`, `expires=`, `runs_remaining=` or a volume budget.

**The precedents for money.** Apify is the closest working analogue to "sell a step and charge per use": publishers define billable events (a result, a run start), **Apify keeps 20% commission** and charges nothing if the Actor earns nothing, payouts need KYC, a quality score penalizes opaque pricing, rental Actors are fully retired on October 1, 2026, and agentic payments arrived in 2026 — per-request via Skyfire and prepaid via "Apify AGI over x402 or MPP" — but **only for Actors with limited permissions**: the trust bar for an agent-paid service is sandboxing, not reputation ([Apify monetize](https://docs.apify.com/actors/publishing/monetize), [Apify pay-per-event](https://docs.apify.com/actors/publishing/monetize/pay-per-event)). Hugging Face's Inference Providers route 200+ models with "pay-as-you-go pricing … with no markup from Hugging Face" and pay model authors nothing — the hub monetizes compute routing, not artifacts ([HF pricing](https://huggingface.co/docs/inference-providers/en/pricing)). In the MCP registry **54.8% of 30,375 servers are remote-only** — a service you call — against 38.9% installable packages ([DevToolHub](https://devtoolhub.com/mcp-registry-by-the-numbers/)). Val Town is the hosted-function community that made the fork path first-class: a val is "a collaborative, versioned folder of deployed code", you "remix another user's val to make changes, then send a pull request", and the docs state no upstream-pull for a remix; Glitch, the remix-culture original, ended hosting on July 8, 2025 citing the cost of "running millions of apps" and abuse, and pointed users at Val Town among others ([docs.val.town](https://docs.val.town/vals/), [Glitch blog](https://blog.glitch.com/post/changes-are-coming-to-glitch/)).

**Rail choice, with numbers.** x402 (USDC, HTTP 402, a facilitator verifies and settles) has the only third-party-measured scale: ~130 million transactions by mid-May 2026, 1,853 resource servers, a Linux Foundation foundation since 2026-04-02; the CDP facilitator is free for 1,000 settlements a month then $0.001 each, with P95 verify 60 ms and settle 900 ms on Base ([arXiv 2605.30998](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30998), [CDP FAQ](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/x402/support/faq), [CDP: x402 Foundation](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/x402/support/x402-foundation)). It also has a June 2026 security analysis documenting four flaw classes a stateless server cannot fix alone — cross-resource substitution (the signature binds payee, value and nonce but not the resource; "all four official SDKs failed resource binding"), a duplicate-settlement race at the CDP facilitator, allowance overdraft on `upto`, and denial of settlement ([arXiv 2605.30998](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30998)). Stripe and Tempo's MPP launched 2026-03-18 as a rail-agnostic 402 envelope (`WWW-Authenticate: Payment …`, `Authorization: Payment …`, `Payment-Receipt`) with `charge`, `session` and `subscription` intents and a Lightning method (BOLT11 charges, prepaid sessions); Aperture v0.5.0's dashboard already labels "L402 and MPP agentic payments" ([mpp.dev](https://mpp.dev), [MPP HTTP transport](https://mpp.dev/protocol/transports/http), [aperture v0.5.0](https://github.com/lightninglabs/aperture/releases/tag/v0.5.0)). Cloudflare's `deferred` scheme is the precedent for "verify an HTTP signature now, settle later in aggregate" with no per-call round trip ([Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/x402/)). For a Lightning shop the fee math is what decides the shape: routing is ~100–143 ppm plus a base fee reported at 0.4–1 sat, so a 10-sat payment loses roughly a tenth to the base fee; Breez's Liquid backend adds ~47 sats per receive and Olympus charges 50% under 20 sats ([spark.money fee market](https://www.spark.money/research/lightning-routing-fee-market-equilibrium), [spark.money LSP comparison](https://www.spark.money/tools/lightning-service-provider-comparison)). The consequence is that **per-call means per-invoice only for expensive calls; cheap calls want a prepaid macaroon for N runs** (a counter is a small piece of state) or a Cashu token for sub-sat units. Lightning Labs' February 2026 agent kit has an L402 client (`lnget`, with a `--max-cost` ceiling, caching a token per domain) labeled "early alpha" and an L402 SDK with TypeScript bindings; the notes confirmed no TypeScript server-side verifier equivalent to Aperture ([Lightning Labs blog](https://lightning.engineering/posts/2026-02-11-ln-agent-tools/), [Lightning Labs Telegram](https://t.me/s/lightninglabs/62)).

**How it serves the four needs.** (1) Distribute like git: it does not — nothing is copied, which is the point and the limit; the buyer cannot read, fork or evolve what they rent. (2) Share patterns: through the peer's `GET /workflows` and `peer: true` listing, which is a catalog of what can be called, not what can be learned from. (3) Sell packages: it reframes selling as access — a year of calls instead of a year of updates — and the same macaroon with a `package:` scope gates the §6 archive route, so one token type covers both. (4) Charge per call: directly and with the strongest precedent of any option; the seller bills the buyer's principal, the buyer's own strut can carry the buyer's Mothership credential on the launch so the LLM spend lands on the buyer, which federation §2.2 defers exactly.

**What it changes in the plan.** It moves §6's "later, in its own plan — paid execution" forward, which **revisits the ruling that what is sold is packages**; the two are not exclusive, and §6 already says both "sit beside this plan without changing a line of §1–§5". It adds a 402 to the launch route for `peer: true` workflows, an `entitlements.json` the buyer's strut already needed for §6, and a client that pays a bolt11 — Sphinx's relay already creates invoices and sends keysend through LND, so the wallet is in the owner's own stack ([stakwork/sphinx-relay](https://github.com/stakwork/sphinx-relay)). "Installing is a person's act" is sidestepped (nothing is installed), but a run an *agent* pays for needs a spend policy a person set — a macaroon budget caveat — and the Apify rule says the paid thing must be sandboxed, which for strut means the sealed and `peer: true` gates plus "as published, no params".

**Maintenance.** This is the inverse of no-maintenance: the seller's strut must be online for every call, and it needs a Lightning receive path (a node, phoenixd at 0.4% flat, Breez, or a custodian over NIP-47 Nostr Wallet Connect, which drives a remote wallet's `make_invoice`/`lookup_invoice` over relays) ([NIP-47](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/47.md)). The cloud strut, not the laptop, is the seller. **Risks.** A stolen L402 is usable by anyone and the server cannot tell, so TLS is mandatory; revoking by root-key rotation kills every token under it, hence the 32-byte `user_id` and a denylist ([L402 spec](https://docs.lightning.engineering/the-lightning-network/l402/protocol-specification)). Metering's own economics: HF pays authors nothing and Apify takes a fifth — a two-strut model has no platform cut, which is good for sellers and means no one is paid to curate. **Maturity call: the mechanism is production (Aperture runs Lightning Loop), the Lightning tooling for agents is months old, and the business model is the only one in the agent world with repeatable third-party income.**

## Option F — a signed static index and a transparency log: the boring baseline with mirrors and tamper evidence

**The idea.** Keep §7's `index.yaml` but make it what apt and F-Droid have run for decades: a signed list on any dumb host, mirrored by anyone, trusted by a pinned key. apt's `Release` file lists hashes of `Packages`, which lists package hashes; `InRelease` is signed by the archive key; "mirrors cannot alter content undetected" ([SecureApt](https://wiki.debian.org/SecureApt)). F-Droid's repo is a `repo/` directory on "a public webserver" with a signed index, added by URL plus key fingerprint; Homebrew's tap is any git URL ([fmd-foss guide](https://fmd-foss.org/security/signing/signing_fdroid_repo), [docs.brew.sh](https://docs.brew.sh/Taps)). Add a transparency log so "same name, different bytes" is detectable: Go's checksum database is an append-only Merkle log with signed tree heads where a mismatch makes the build "complain loudly on standard error and fail" and proxies can front and mirror it ([Go sumdb design](https://go.googlesource.com/proposal/+/master/design/25530-sumdb.md)); Sigstore's Rekor v2 went GA 2025-10-10 as a "tile-backed transparency log" with self-hostable POSIX, MySQL/S3 and Spanner backends, though the public instance kept v1 as default in June 2026 over client breaking changes and shards every ~6 months ([Sigstore blog](https://blog.sigstore.dev/), [rekor-tiles](https://github.com/sigstore/rekor-tiles)). `sigstore-js` is the library npm's own provenance runs on ([sigstore-js](https://github.com/sigstore/sigstore-js)).

**How it serves the four needs.** (1) Distribute like git: a mirrorable index gives any-host fetch without touching the fork model — this option is orthogonal to the fork problem and does not dissolve it. (2) Share patterns: discovery stays out-of-band, as it is for apt and F-Droid; the AUR shows what "votes as trust" buys — a malicious `google-chrome-stable` lived "only for a few hours" yet "did get a few upvotes" ([EndeavourOS forum](https://forum.endeavouros.com/t/malicious-aur-packages/80095?page=20)). (3) Sell: a private index is a private shop, same as §6's private repo. (4) Nothing.

**What it changes in the plan.** Sign `index.yaml` and the archive; log digests to a tile log the deployment runs or to public Rekor. **Maintenance.** A static host; a log if you want one. **Risks.** Key rotation history in apt — expiring keys broke installers in 2007, dual-signing during transitions ([SecureApt](https://wiki.debian.org/SecureApt)). **Maturity call: production for decades; solves integrity and mirroring, not discovery or forks; cheapest hardening of what the plan already has.**

## Agent-ecosystem precedents: callable units get reused, metered execution gets paid, and free registration gets gamed

**The unit that gets reused is the one with a runtime contract.** The official MCP registry — metadata only, "deliberately unopinionated", still in preview — held **30,375 unique servers by September 2026**, tripling since May, with 54.8% remote-only; but 62% have exactly one published version, 16,356 of ~18,200 publishers have one server, a single account holds 1,505 servers of "mostly single-function micro-tools", 276 entries share an identical description, and "the registry adds; it does not prune" ([DevToolHub](https://devtoolhub.com/mcp-registry-by-the-numbers/), [MCP registry about](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/registry/about)). n8n shows the split cleanly: ~5.3K community node *packages* are dependencies with semver, ~8.5K templates are free copies drawing on only 333 unique node types, and from May 1, 2026 verified nodes must be built by GitHub Actions with a provenance statement ([n8n-stats](https://n8n-stats.gui.do/nodes), [n8n-pulse](https://n8n-pulse.gui.do/templates), [n8n verification](https://docs.n8n.io/connect/create-nodes/build-your-node/reference/verification-guidelines)). ComfyUI is the one registry that records the node version *inside the workflow file* so a shared workflow reproduces — the analogue of strut's `type@vN` and `run.start.stepHashes` ([docs.comfy.org](https://docs.comfy.org/registry/overview)). Agent Skills — a folder with SKILL.md — won breadth (30–40 tools adopted it within six months of the December 2025 open standard) because the "runtime" is the model reading markdown, and for the same reason they have no monetization ([noqta.tn](https://noqta.tn/en/blog/agent-skills-skill-md-universal-ai-coding-standard-2026)). Claude Code's plugin marketplace is the lowest-friction model in the field: a JSON file in a git repo, a new copy "only when the plugin's computed version changes", auto-update off by default, pin by `sha`, release channels as two marketplaces, no deprecation state — deliberately dumb, and it has not stopped adoption ([Host a marketplace](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/plugins/host-marketplace)). The mapping for strut is direct: **steps are the dependency unit, workflows are the copy unit, Concepts are skills**; the plan's "wrap rather than edit" nudge for steps and "fork freely" ruling for apps match how the field actually behaves.

**Almost nobody tracks upstream for a modified copy.** The working answers are depend-and-pin (ComfyUI, Claude plugins, npm-based nodes), remix-and-PR (Val Town, whose remix pages show lineage and counts but no documented upstream pull), or re-copy the template (n8n, Dify) ([docs.val.town](https://docs.val.town/vals/)). Auto-updating something you approved is the rug-pull threat, not a feature ([CSA whitepaper](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research-rb/csa-whitepaper-mcp-security-tool-poisoning-20260506-csa-styl/)). That is the strongest external support for the owner's "updates ask" ruling, and the strongest argument that Option A's coexistence — not Option D's convergence — is the fork answer.

**Where money flows.** Through metered hosted execution billed by a platform: Apify's 20% on publisher-defined events; Poe's up to $20 per referred subscriber plus creator-set per-message prices; HF's pass-through inference billing ([Apify monetize](https://docs.apify.com/actors/publishing/monetize), [TechCrunch on Poe](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/25/quoras-poe-now-lets-users-create-and-share-custom-ai-powered-apps), [HF pricing](https://huggingface.co/docs/inference-providers/en/pricing)). Content revenue-share promises did not materialize: the GPT Store promised engagement-based payouts in Q1 2024 and by late 2025 "hasn't since provided many updates", and the ChatGPT App Directory forbids selling digital goods or subscriptions in-app ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=2647464), [Moburst](https://www.moburst.com/blog/chatgpt-app-directory-explained-what-brands-need-to-know/)). Package sales for workflows exist only as off-platform side businesses (n8n template clubs). Decentralized agent economies show registration ≫ activity ≫ payment: Virtuals ACP went from **$345,197 in platform fees in March 2026 to $5.48 in September**, its v2 across four chains did ~$2,660 of total volume since April, and of 44,052 registered agents 2,470 ever had a job and 151 earned over $1,000; of 15,484 tokens launched in one week, a random sample of 150 found three that ever traded ([chainward.ai](https://chainward.ai/decodes/virtuals-top-dog)). Bittensor's ~$17M annualized revenue sits against a critic's ~$323M a year in emissions — "emission-driven, not demand-driven" ([FalconX](https://falconx.io/newsroom/state-of-bittensor-subnet-adoption-trends-network-mechanics-and-covenants-departure), [sawinyh.com](https://sawinyh.com/blog/bittensor-tao-tokenomics/)). Fountain's value-for-value streaming — "a few sats per minute" — sent $127,900 to creators in 2024, real but small ([Fountain 2024 review](https://typefully.com/teemupleb/fountain-earn-bitcoin-by-listening-to-JlddRdG), [Juraj Bednár](https://juraj.bednar.io/en/blog-en/2024/10/23/how-i-save-my-time-and-attention-with-value4value-podcasts/)). The lesson for a strut marketplace: **measure "ran and paid", not "registered"; expect discovery, not protocol, to be the gate; and expect the money to be in Option E.**

**The trust signals that survived 2026 are all costly to fake**: a controlled domain or GitHub namespace, a reproducible build with provenance, a sandboxed run with a payment attached, a conformance-checked manifest. Vanity metrics were gamed wherever they were free; A2A's self-published Agent Cards reached 0.29% of crawled API hosts with 10 of 65 conformant ([API Evangelist](https://apievangelist.com/2026/07/29/most-published-agent-cards-are-not-actually-a2a/)). Strut's claims/checks/evidence per run, `stepHashes`, sealed namespaces and the `publisher: ai` stamp line up with what worked, and the notable missing precedent — a public *run-backed* quality score like Apify's — is exactly what §7 says an index cannot yet carry. Agents consuming shared logic is mainstream (skills loaded mid-task, actors paid by agents via x402 or Skyfire, Microsoft's agents fetching skills from an MCP server at runtime); agents publishing is visible only as floods that degraded the registry they landed in, which is why 2026 enterprise registries added approval workflows ([Microsoft devblogs](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/agent-framework/discover-agent-skills-from-mcp-servers-in-net/), [AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/agent-registry/latest/APIReference/Welcome.md)). Strut's answer is already in place: let agents call and pay freely once the service is sandboxed; make agent publishing go through the same stamped, hash-pinned, person-accepted path.

## Recommendation: coexistence as the spine, Nostr to announce, L402 to charge, in stages

**Stage 0 — make Option A the stated model, now, with no new network.** The plan has the digest and the `upstream` stamp; what it lacks is the stance. Write §4 so that "the new version lands beside the fork and the fork stays active" is the normal outcome rather than the exception row, name the per-node name→hash map as such, and add a schema-diff check to `update_package` that classifies each re-point as silent (schemas and claims unchanged under every dependent) or needs-a-person before the ask — Elm's `elm bump` logic over Zod schemas. Drop the implied expectation that the way back upstream is a PR into the publisher's repo: a fork that is good is published as its own hash with its `upstream`, and the publisher may re-point to it or not. Every existing ruling holds — installing is a person's act, updates ask, no DRM — and nothing changes in §1–§3.

**Stage 1 — announce and fetch by hash (Option B's events on Option F's discipline), with Option C for laptops.** Add a signed release record per published package version in the three-event shape (application, release, asset-with-`x`) and a `nostr:` resolver that reads the publisher's write relays and fetches by sha256 with BUD-03-style fallback across the publisher's server list and the strut's own archive route; sign `index.yaml` and the archive at the same time. Keep the "no public index yet" ruling intact by having each deployment name its relays as it names its indexes — an org relay plus a paid relay is not a public index; relay.damus.io is. State the reason for choosing Nostr over Pkarr-first honestly: it is the only option with announce *and* social discovery *and* mature TypeScript libraries, and its sha256-by-URL blob model is what the archive route already does. Add Pkarr as a `pk:` source and an optional public key on the peer record when the desktop-behind-NAT case needs it; choose iroh for the connection only if its blob layer has stabilized by then, otherwise Hyperswarm or a Pkarr relay plus strut's existing HTTP is enough. Flagged gaps the owner should know before building: no 2024–2026 Mainline DHT size measurement; Zapstore's kinds are the project's label rather than a merged NIP; no source on how Nostr clients defend against a relay serving a stale release event beyond multi-relay reads; Nostr key loss has no protocol answer, so the pasted peer record stays the root of trust for which key is current.

**Stage 2 — charge with a 402 on both routes (Option E).** Put an L402-shaped challenge on the `peer: true` launch route and on the §6 archive route, with macaroon caveats strut already knows how to mint and verify: `workflow=`, `package=`, `expires=`, a `user_id` for revocation, a budget for prepaid runs. Sell expensive runs per invoice and cheap runs as prepaid N-run tokens, because the Lightning base fee dominates cent-sized payments; keep a counter as the one piece of state. Adopt MPP's header vocabulary only if being legible to MPP clients and Aperture's dashboard matters; the shape is the same. Carry the buyer's Mothership credential on the launch so LLM spend lands on the buyer, as federation §2.2 defers. This revisits the ruling "what is sold is packages": it adds access as a second thing sold without removing the first, and §6 already reserved the slot. Gaps: no TypeScript L402 server-side verifier was confirmed (strut would write its own over the macaroon primitives it has), no L402 adoption metrics exist, and `lnget` is "early alpha".

**Stage 3 — discovery and a track record, later.** Curation sets from followed keys and a WoT score are the pattern feed; a run-backed score — claims supported or refuted per digest on other struts, labelled *reported* — is what §7 says an index cannot yet hold and what no open registry has. Only then is a public index worth revisiting. Use Automerge (Option D) for Concept pages and curation lists if several struts' builders start editing the same pages; never for code.

What this leaves alone is deliberate: `git+` and `file:` sources stay for deployments that want nothing social; the three-way merge with the builder resolving markers and the claims re-running stays the convergence tool; a fleet distro stays a branch of a repo. The research does not support replacing git — it supports no longer making git's branch-owner model the only way a change travels between struts.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the owner's problem. "Avoid the fork/PR treadmill" sounded like a transport question — which network replaces GitHub — and turned out to be an identity question strut has nearly answered already: once a name is admitted to be per-node metadata over a content hash, upstream changing and you changing are two leaves, and the only thing left to design is the typed re-point and who may announce a new hash. Everything peer-to-peer in the notes then reduces to four mechanisms strut can adopt one at a time (republishable signed records, stateless relays, fetch-by-hash from any holder, offline-verifiable tokens) rather than a stack to bet on, and the systems that tried to be a whole stack — IPFS pinning, SSB pubs, Glitch's free hosting, NIP-90 marketplaces — are the ones that decayed.

The second shift is about money. Every ecosystem with public numbers monetizes the call, not the copy, and the one place package-style selling works is where the thing sold is updates and evidence to people who could fork anyway — which is exactly the owner's no-DRM ruling stated as a business model. That makes strut's existing run-on-a-peer path, with a macaroon it can already verify from a public key, the shortest road to the fourth need, and it means the honest success metric for the whole effort is not packages listed or struts registered but runs that were paid for.
